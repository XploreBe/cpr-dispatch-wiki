02 · Business & organization track

How the work was run, what it produced, what it cost

The same three weeks, read for the questions a team lead or a budget-holder asks: how were people organized, how much did they ship, what did it cost, and what risks does this way of working carry. These are observations from a testbed, not a sales case.

The operating model

The greenfield restart adopted a deliberately light structure, designed around the retrospective's lessons rather than around a large-project template.

  • Effort follows expertise, not a fixed owner. Everyone develops features end-to-end across the repos, but we're stronger in some technologies than others, so we lean into the ones we know best — especially for review and for setting the conventions and rules — while still giving and receiving feedback on the rest.
  • Agree the contract before splitting the work. There is no fixed sprint ceremony; we develop as we go and meet once there's a plan written down. Before any work is split, the shared contract (endpoints, shapes, events, error codes) is agreed and pinned in a shared artifact — the direct mitigation of "agree the contract before parallelizing."
  • Each repo keeps its own harness. Structural changes to shared pieces are agreed by everyone; touching them mid-sprint without warning is out, because it destabilizes others' work.
  • Zero premature complexity. A greenfield restart is the perfect temptation to build "the definitive harness" on day 1; the explicit antidote is that every new convention must be born from a friction already felt, not anticipated.

The three-week organizational arc

The shape of the team changed every week — and output rose as it got smaller, because the method, not the headcount, was doing the work.

  • Week 1 — the team, fully paired
    Planning, implementation, and review done together. Slower by design, but everything was agreed and no rework came from a misunderstanding between the pair. Established that the process is resumable by a different person.
  • Week 2 — two parallel streams
    For the first time, two streams ran at once, one person each, split by application. The main friction was the "restack" — when one stream merges, the other's stack must be brought up to date. Working in different applications avoided collisions.
  • Week 3 — one developer, separate QA
    All application work ran through a single developer with several AI work-streams at once; testing sat with a separate QA person on a different project, raising bugs and enhancements. Highest-output week of the three.

Output rose across the three weeks — 21 → 33 → 57 pull requests — while the team went from two people to one. The stated reason is the way of working: the machine reviews every change and runs the safety checks, so a change can merge without a person reading every line by hand. Fifty-seven finished, reviewed changes from one developer in a week is not achievable with code written and reviewed entirely by hand.

Cost

Cost visibility was the most uncomfortable gap in the process for the first two weeks. The Claude Code token was created directly in the terminal rather than through the console where an API key would be issued, so there was no per-run or per-week spend to look at. Every cost decision in week 2 — one review pass instead of two, the stronger model instead of the cheaper one — was made without the number.

In week 3, because it was one developer on one machine, usage could be read straight from that machine's sessions — the full cost of building the app that week, not a sample. How to read the dollar figure

It is an estimate at standard model prices, to give it a scale a budget-holder recognizes; the team pays through a flat subscription, so there is no per-item bill. Most of the usage is the model re-reading the same context as it works through stacked changes — by far the cheapest part. The headline: a finished, tested, reviewed change costs on the order of twenty-five dollars of model usage.

Two cost findings carry over from the earlier controlled experiment (see the experiment reference): the money is not mainly in the model that writes the code — planning, coordinating, and re-verifying dominate — and a cheaper implementation model was not cheaper at this stage, because it needed more attempts and more human re-checking to reach the same bar.

Risks we can see

  • Expertise gaps don't disappear. Nobody on the week-1 pair had done an AWS deployment before; that work still got produced. If you don't want to trust everything to the AI, you still have to learn the subject, or wrong things can pass unseen.
  • Parallel streams can clash. Week 3 had one case where several AI work-streams collided and needed a quick fix to main — small this time, but the kind of thing to watch as more work runs in parallel.
  • The QA↔development loop needs to scale. QA and development sit in different projects; the findings flow needs to be cleaner as the team grows.

What this suggests — as observations

Reading, not recommendation

At an early stage, the lever is not the AI on its own — it is human organization, experiment isolation, and convention maturity. The tooling and the models pay off once the project has enough mass and structure to support them.

  • On future team shapes: a small team plus a trustworthy pipeline can produce at a rate normally associated with a larger one — provided the safety checks stay strong and the work stays resumable. The constraint that replaces headcount is review trust and context hygiene, not the number of hands.
  • On cost: at this scale the dominant cost is human-in-the-loop orchestration, not model tokens; the model tier barely moves the total. Cheaper models become interesting only once conventions are mature enough to make them safe — a "not yet," revisited as the codebase grows.
  • On applicability to client work: the transferable asset is the way of working — an instrumented, traceable pipeline with plan-as-state and deterministic gates — more than any single application. Whether that generalizes beyond a testbed is exactly what the ongoing work is meant to find out.
